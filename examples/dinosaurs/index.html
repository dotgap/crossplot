The Long Dig

For two centuries, people have pulled dinosaurs out of riverbanks, deserts and quarry walls. The 21,253 finds they wrote down tell a story of their own, about deep time, lucky rock and the hunters who went looking.

IIn the badlands of eastern Montana, one summer storm can wash a Triceratops horn out of a hillside. The bone lies in the gully, gray and cracked, 66 million years after the animal died on a warm coastal plain. In 1902 a fossil hunter named Barnum Brown worked these same gullies and dug out the first skeleton of Tyrannosaurus rex.

That skeleton, and more than 21,000 other finds, now sit in the Paleobiology Database, a record kept by scientists around the world. Read together, they show when dinosaurs lived, and how lucky we are to know them at all.

A record written in lucky rock

Dinosaurs ruled for some 165 million years, from about 233 million years ago until an asteroid struck what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, 66 million years ago. But the rock that holds their bones was never laid down evenly, and much of it has since been ground to dust. Half of all finds come from the last 35 million years alone.

That swell on the right is not a population boom. It is geography: a few rock layers so rich that paleontologists return to them summer after summer. Dinosaur Park in Alberta, where the eroding badlands of the Red Deer River have given up some of the most complete skeletons ever found. Two Medicine and Judith River in Montana. And at the very end, Hell Creek, Lance and the Nemegt of Mongolia. Pick the last few million years and you are standing in the world of .

The smaller peak, around 150 million years ago, is mostly one layer too: the Morrison Formation, a vast sheet of ancient river sand that stretches from Montana to New Mexico. It is the land of the giants, and the long-necks surge.

Teeth, claws and beaks

Theropods, the two-legged hunters that ranged from crow-sized raptors to the 12-meter Tyrannosaurus, lead the count by a wide margin. Part of the reason is in their mouths. A theropod shed and regrew teeth all its life, scattering them like litter, and some of the most common names in the database, such as Richardoestesia and Paronychodon, are known almost only from those lost teeth.

Where the bones come out of the ground

Three in ten finds come from the United States. That reflects geology and history more than where dinosaurs actually roamed. The badlands of Montana, Wyoming and Utah expose exactly the right rocks, and collectors have worked them since the 1870s, when the bitter rivals Edward Drinker Cope and Othniel Charles Marsh raced each other across the West. Today much of the new growth comes from China, Argentina and Brazil.

The United States has led in every period. Follow the other lines, though. Britain, third before 1950, has slipped to ninth, while Spain climbed from near the bottom to second place in the 2000s and 2010s. In the 2020s, Argentina and Brazil are the risers.

A world on the move

Every dot on this map is a place where bones, teeth, eggs or footprints came out of the ground. The clusters trace the badlands of the American West, the Gobi, the red cliffs of Patagonia and the farm country of northeastern China: places where the right rock lies at the surface and someone came looking.

Buried in a flood

A dinosaur became a fossil only if something buried it fast, before scavengers and weather could pull it apart. Rivers in flood did that best, smothering carcasses in sand and mud, so most finds come from ancient channels and floodplains. A few hundred lie in rock laid down in the open sea: bodies that drifted out, bloated, and sank.

The hunters

In 1824 the English geologist and clergyman William Buckland described a jaw lined with blade-like teeth and named its owner Megalosaurus, the first dinosaur ever named. For a century and a half afterward, finds came in a trickle. Then the pace exploded: six in ten finds in the database were published after 2000, many of them from China, where in 1996 a farmer in Liaoning Province split open a slab and found Sinosauropteryx, the first dinosaur known to have feathers.

The lonely spike in 1865 is a single book: the catalogue of Edward Hitchcock's collection at Amherst College, published by his son the year after he died. Hitchcock spent decades gathering slabs of Connecticut Valley sandstone printed with three-toed tracks, which he believed giant birds had made. About 2,400 finds in the database are footprints like his, and 1,000 are eggs.
